## x. Objects

A very common word in Kotava is **va**.

You use **va** to mark that the noun following it is the object of the action, i.e. the noun the action is happening to or acting upon.  The noun following **va** is called the 'object complement.'

​	**Va sumewisiki wir.** He's looking at the television.

​	**Va in kiaval.** You're greeting him.

### x.1. Transitive vs. intransitive

A verb (or other word acting as a verb, like certain participles[^1] that does an action directly on something using **va** is called '<u>transitive</u>'. In English, examples are: to bind, to rip, to nourish, to unwrap. The opposite, an action that doesn't take an object directly like that, is called '<u>intransitive</u>'. Some English examples are: to sit, to ripen, to stay. Depending on the context, some English verbs can be either transitive or intransitive, e.g. to escape (I escaped. I escaped the house.), to sing (I was singing. I sang a song.), to see (I can see! Did you see me?), or to write (I like to write. I am writing a book.)

Verbs are not marked as transitive in the dictionary, but you can get a sense from the meaning, other words from the same root ([Kotapedia](https://kotava.org/kotapedia) is great for this) and also from reading in Kotava which ones are used that way.

#### x.1.1 Making a verb transitive

Starting with a verb that doesn't take a direct object (an 'intransitive' verb), you can make it transitive by adding the suffix **-si** to the root.

​	**KENIBE** (to sleep) ➡️ **KENIBESI** (to put sb/sth to sleep)

​	**LUBE** (to fall) ➡️ **LUBESI** (to fell sth, to make sb/sth fall)

​	**AELE** (to hunger) ➡️ **AELESI** (to starve sb/sth)

Since **-si** is added to the *root*, the personal endings are added on to it:

​	**WIDLA** (to shake, to move (int.))

​	➡️ **WIDLASI** (to shake sb/sth, to move sb/sth)

​	**... nume ine[^2] skotcapar eke va mona *widlasir*.**..

​	*[so] [she] [she-trembles-big] [to-such-an-extent] [obj >] [mona] [she-shakes-tr.]*

​	... and she trembled till she shook the house... (*[Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11/11-h/11-h.htm#chap04)*)

#### x.1.3. Ditransitive

A verb that is transitive but that takes *two* objects is called 'ditransitive'. In English, we think of these as verbs that take a 'direct object' and an 'indirect object'[^3], e.g. to give, to thank for, to take off of. There aren't many of these.

The direct object is introduced by **va** like normal, and the indirect object is marked by the word **gu**:

**zilí** (to give)

The thing being given gets **va**, and the person it's being given to gets **gu**.

**grewá** (to thank for)

The person being thanked gets **va**, and what they're getting thanked for gets **gu**.

**tiolté** (to take sth off of sth)

The thing being moved gets **va**, and the surface it's being removed from gets **gu**.



[^1]: [x.x](Participles.md)
[^2]: It is believed that **in** gets an extra **-e** here for euphony, since the next word starts with a consonant cluster (**sk-**).
[^3]: In English the indirect object is usually introduced with 'to' or 'for'.
